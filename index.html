Puzzle cube

Five interlocking pieces, 27 unit cubes in total, that assemble into one 3×3×3 cube. I designed the pieces with physical blocks, sketched them by hand, then modeled and assembled them in CAD.

Process

  1. CP1 Brainstorming. Tested physical blocks until I had 5 unique pieces that interlock into the cube. Two pieces were too similar, so I redesigned one.
  2. CP2 Sketches. Isometric and orthographic sketches of every piece. This was the hardest part: I spent 6 hours after school on one drawing, then went to tutorials and it clicked.
  3. CP3 CAD models. Modeled each piece with dimensions. Precision mattered here, because a small error means the pieces don't fit.
  4. CP4 Final assembly. Assembled the cube in CAD and produced an isometric view and an exploded view.

Images

CP2 Sketches

What I learned
  • Anything visible in the isometric view must show up in at least 2 of the 3 orthographic views.
  • CAD sketch, extrude, and assembly tools.
  • The difference between isometric and exploded views.

Not my strongest project, but I stuck with it and asked for help when I was stuck.

Brentwood Appliances TS-213BL coffee maker
Purpose
Make coffee, heat it, and give it a good texture, while saving time on a morning or evening drink.
How it operates
Water flows from the reservoir into tubes, where electric current heats it. It comes out of the showerhead into the brew basket, mixes with the coffee grounds, and drips down into the cup.

Why reverse engineer

Taking a product apart shows its components, elements, principles, and functions. That tells you what can be changed to improve the design without breaking its main purpose.

Collaboration

Next time
  • Take cleaner photos.
  • Keep more organized notes on hidden parts.
  • Use neater line weights.

Toy motor car

Finished
Partner
Trent Warner
Power
6V battery, 3V DC motor
Goal
20 ft, straight line
Final size
9.5 × 4 × 2 in
Style
F1-inspired

A motorized toy car made mostly of cardboard, designed to teach elementary-age kids basic engineering. It had to run on a 6V battery and pulley system and travel at least 20 ft in a straight line.

Design brief

Client
Elementary school students and educators
End user
Elementary-age kids who want engaging, educational toys
Problem
Kids lack affordable, sustainable, interactive toys that teach engineering.
Criteria
Has a battery, uses electrical energy, is creative, and the battery and switch are visible.
Constraints
At most 10 in long, 6 in wide, and 5 in tall including wheels. Cardboard no thicker than ¼ in. No glue, no 3D printing, and only 12 in of duct tape.

Research and sketches

We each studied 5 reference cars, 10 in total, including a prop-driven cardboard RC car, a rubber band car, a solar car, and a propeller toy. Then we sketched four designs at 1:2 scale for CP6: Trent drew an F1 car and a Nissan Skyline, and I drew a Cybertruck and a fan motor car. We chose the F1 style because it's light, quick, uses little material, and looks good.

Build

  1. Base. Cut a cardboard base with folding sides.
  2. First prototype. Built it in the library.
  3. Drivetrain. DC motor to a pulley, a rubber band belt, and a pulley on the rear axle. The axles run through straw bearings.
  4. Problem. Our first working prototype's wheels spun when we lifted it, but it stalled on the floor. The motor's torque was too little to overcome friction between the wheels and the floor, and between the wheels and the body, where they hit the sides.
  5. Finding the cause. For hours we tried different ideas. We found the pulley had too much friction, and the battery holder added a lot of weight.
  6. Fixes. We moved the batteries to the back, over the rear drive wheels, so the car became rear-wheel drive with better traction. We also repositioned the wheels so they no longer rubbed the inside walls, and remeasured and tightened the internal dimensions. After that, the motor finally had enough torque and the car moved consistently.
  7. Race day. The first 2 runs drifted off course or wouldn't start. We kept adjusting wheel alignment and weight balance between runs, and by the later runs the car ran straighter and more reliably. It finished ahead of most other designs.

Final car

What made our car unique
  • An F1-inspired body that was lighter and used less material than most builds.
  • A unique color and styling that set it apart.
  • A low, narrow profile for a straighter, faster run.
What I learned
  • A motor that spins freely in the air can still stall under load. Friction decides whether the torque is enough.
  • Putting weight over the drive wheels adds traction. I worked through this torque problem with Claude while we were building.
Next time
  • Measure carefully up front.
  • Leave more clearance inside the body.
  • Test motor torque against friction before full assembly.

A hand crank drives a worm screw, a gear train, and a chain and sprocket to a spool that pulls the load up an inclined plane. My calculated mechanical advantage is 7.38, inside the required range of 5 to 15. After a rough start, the finished machine worked.

Design brief

Client
Rescue System Inc.
End user
Response team
Problem
Lift pieces of rubble, about 1 kg, 5 in high to see if anything is inside.
Design statement
A machine powered only by hand that can lift a 1 kg weight 5 in high.
Criteria and constraints
  • Mechanical advantage greater than 5 and less than 15
  • Lift 1 kg 5 in vertically
  • At least 5 simple machines: lever, wheel and axle, inclined plane, screw or worm, and a gear train or chain and sprocket
  • A single human input
  • VEX parts only, on one base plate

Planning the build

Power path and MA calculations

  1. Hand crank
    Lever, on a rod through an 18T sprocket
    Lever = 1
  2. Worm screw
    Screw, 7 TPI
    C / pitch = 2.7475
    Worm gears are high friction and self-locking, so expect lower efficiency.
  3. Worm to 60T gear
    Screw mechanism
    60 / 7 = 8.57
  4. Gear train
    60T gear drives 36T gear
    36 / 60 = 0.6
    MA below 1. This stage trades force for speed.
  5. Chain and sprocket
    24T sprocket on the 36T gear's axle, chain to a 24T sprocket
    24 / 24 = 1
    Easiest to calculate, since both sprockets are 24T.
  6. Spool
    Wheel and axle
    0.125 / 0.5 = 0.25
    Hardest to calculate. The parts were tiny and hard to measure, and it was easy to mix up diameter and radius.
  7. Inclined plane
    11 in base, 6 in height, about 12.53 in slope
    12.53 / 6 = 2.09
Total MA = 2.7475 × 1 × 8.57 × 1 × 2.09 × 0.25 × 0.6 = 7.38

Inside the required range of 5 to 15.

What happened

After the first 2 build days, I realized my plan was a disaster. My sketches were 2D, so the design wouldn't meet the criteria. I researched how simple machines are actually used, sketched different views in my notebook, and had Claude build an animated 3D model of the machine. It was wrong at first, but after iterating against the criteria and constraints, we had a buildable plan that worked.

Problems
  • 2D sketches hid whether the design actually met the criteria.
  • We lost a lot of time screwing in rails and keeping gears in place.
What I learned
  • Trial and error taught me how compound machines work. Now I notice them in cranes and construction.
  • From my mentorship this summer: companies split features across teams, and the systems engineer oversees the merge.
Working as a team
  • We changed where parts went many times, and that iteration got us to a working machine.
  • I listened first, then gave my input, and we had no arguments about the build steps.
  • When ideas conflict, a decision matrix is the fairest way to settle it.
Next time
  • Plan better, and be conceptually right from the start.
  • Focus early on the parts that need the most stability.
